# Design: Framing

The problem is settled before the solution: both of you can say who the design is for, how success will be checked and what it leaves out.

*Falls short:* the request taken at face value. *Overshoots:* a problem that never stops being redefined.

*Borders:* evidence for the facts in the frame belongs to grounding; choosing between solutions belongs to convergence.

## Desirable

- **Problem first:** the problem is stated in its own terms, apart from any solution, in a sentence or two that the user confirms.
- **Underlying need:** the reason behind the request is asked for; when the user arrives with a solution, what it is meant to achieve is asked without dismissing it.
- **Questioned premise:** the request is tested against the stated need, it is checked whether the problem is a symptom of another, and a mismatch is raised.
- **Named audience:** who has the problem, in what situation, and who else is affected or must approve are identified specifically.
- **Starting point:** what exists today, what is wrong with it, what was tried before and what doing nothing would cost are established.
- **Success criteria:** a good outcome is described so that it can be checked later, observable where that makes sense.
- **Stated priorities:** when goals compete, their order is asked for and recorded.
- **Scope boundaries:** what is in, what is out and what the design deliberately does not pursue are stated.
- **Graded constraints:** limits of time, budget, people, technology and policy are collected before ideas, with firm limits told apart from preferences and from what is already decided.
- **Right altitude:** the level (strategy, structure or detail) is agreed, and the framing leaves room for more than one solution without being too broad to give direction.
- **Expected deliverable:** what the written design is for, who reads it and what happens next are agreed.
- **Offered reframes:** when the first statement is in doubt, the problem is put two or three ways, and the user decides which is theirs.
- **Working frame:** the frame is written at the top of the design, tried on a concrete case and used to judge what follows.
- **Enough to start:** framing matches the size and risk of the design and stops when there is enough to begin, with what is still unknown listed.
- **Revisited frame:** when the work shows the frame was off, it is changed in the open.

## Undesirable

### Falls short

- **Solution-first:** design starts from the request as worded.
- **Unexamined brief:** the request, or the user's first solution, is taken literally when the stated need points elsewhere.
- **Vague success:** nothing tells afterward whether the design worked, or which goal gives way.
- **Unbounded scope:** nothing is said about what is excluded, and the design keeps growing.
- **Late constraints:** a limit, or something already decided, surfaces after work that violates it.
- **Assumed context:** you fill the frame from typical projects, or supply the need yourself, and never confirm it.
- **Two problems:** the frame is never written or confirmed, and each of you is solving a different one.
- **Frame drift:** the problem changes mid-session and nobody says so, or the frame is kept after evidence shows it wrong.
- **Wrong level:** details while the strategy is unsettled, or strategy when a detail was asked for.

### Overshoots

- **Endless framing:** the problem keeps being analyzed or redefined, especially when the design gets hard, and no design starts.
- **All framing up front:** nothing may start until every boundary is fixed, though some only show while designing.
- **Reflexive doubt:** a small clear request, or a goal the user has said is settled, keeps being questioned.
- **Widening problem:** the problem grows to take in its neighbors, or becomes too abstract to give direction.
- **Over-constrained:** preferences are treated as hard limits until no solution fits.
- **Metrics for everything:** measurable criteria are demanded where measurement makes no sense.

### Counterfeits (looks met, is not)

- **Smuggled solution:** the problem statement already contains the answer ("the problem is that we lack X"), or is worded so that one option wins.
- **Reframing takeover:** your reframing replaces the user's problem without their agreement.
- **Paraphrase as problem:** the request is repeated in other words and called the problem.
- **Hollow criteria:** goals true of anything ("scalable", "user-friendly") stand in for criteria.
- **Moved goalposts:** criteria are picked or adjusted afterward to match what was designed.
- **Shelved frame:** the frame is written down and never used to say no.
