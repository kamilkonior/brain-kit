# Retro

## Intent

Turn what a piece of work just taught into a lasting, verified upgrade to the working system, together with the user — starting from a concrete episode that failed, dragged, recurred, or went unusually well, identifying the systemic conditions behind it and whether its recurrence or stakes justify changing how future work runs, and landing the improvement in the lightest durable substrate that governs the behaviour across repo checks, scripts, templates, agent tools, skills, memory, decision agreements, or trigger-backed habits. Changes stay small, self-contained, and cleanly separated from in-flight product work, leave the working system lighter or sharper rather than merely more crowded, and are shown to act; improvements big enough to be engineering projects of their own are promoted into the pipeline instead of improvised inside retro. Retro ends when the upgrade is in place — or the signal is deliberately left alone for now — the user has seen the evidence and the change, can say in their own words what will be different from now on and for whom, and any product repair is routed separately, returning to the work under the improved setup.

It falls short when retro stops at discussion, blame, or hollow action items in chat, normalises recurring drag, ignores quiet wins, picks fragile reminders where a check or tool tweak was cheap, or leaves the change untested. It overshoots when it reacts to one-off noise with rigid rules, piles on process and context bloat without pruning, runs a shadow build for a large tooling project, silently modifies skills or repo rules without consent, or drifts into fixing product code inside retro.

#### Attribution

The lesson rests on concrete evidence, names the systemic conditions behind it, and weighs whether its recurrence or stakes warrant changing the working system.

*Falls short:* blame, vibes, normalising chronic drag, or ignoring what went well. *Overshoots:* turning a one-off fluke into a sweeping law, or forensic archaeology past what is actionable.

Why: a lesson aimed at the wrong cause or thin noise adds permanent drag; one missed lets the same friction return.

**Desirable**

- **Double-loop learning:** questions the rule, tool, assumption or setup that produced the outcome, not only the single slip within it.
- **Blameless postmortem:** treats failures as properties of the system, tooling or information available at the time, not personal fault.
- **Contributing factors:** names the conditions that made the friction or failure easy, or the win repeatable, rather than pointing at one scapegoat.
- **Bright spots:** captures what went unusually smoothly so effective practices are repeated by design, not left accidental.
- **Watchful waiting:** when evidence is thin or likely a one-off, records the signal and holds off changing the working system until recurrence justifies it.

**Undesirable**

- **Normalisation of deviance:** tolerating chronic friction, noisy checks or recurring workarounds until they feel normal.
- **Fighting the last war:** installing a rigid guardrail tailored to the exact shape of one past incident that will not repeat the same way.
- **Hasty generalisation:** turning a narrow episode or context-dependent win into sweeping dogma that burdens unrelated work.
- **Hindsight bias:** judging what happened as if the outcome had been obvious beforehand, hiding what actually misled the work at the time.
- **Human error:** concluding that the user or agent should simply remember or be more careful next time without changing the system.

#### Leverage

Each chosen upgrade lands in the lightest durable substrate that governs the behaviour, costs future work less than it saves, and stays bounded to a small meta-change.

*Falls short:* hollow takeaways that vanish on exit, or weak willpower reminders where a check or tool tweak was cheap. *Overshoots:* rule bloat, heavy machinery for soft judgment, or turning retro into a shadow build or product fix.

Why: a lesson nobody carries forward changes nothing, and a heavy guard paid on every task soon costs more than the occasional mistake.

**Desirable**

- **Hierarchy of controls:** picks the strongest proportionate substrate: eliminate the friction or automate a check or tool first; use a skill, instruction, template, agreement or trigger-backed habit only where judgment belongs.
- **Poka-yoke:** changes the repo, tool, schema or check so the bad path is prevented or caught automatically rather than left to memory.
- **Forcing function:** places the check or cue directly in the path of the work where it will actually fire, not in a document someone must remember to open.
- **Net positive:** the change saves more future attention, context or effort than it adds, preferring to remove friction or prune stale rules over piling on new ones.

**Undesirable**

- **Good intentions:** ending in chat notes, action items or promises to watch out next time, with nothing changed in the repo, tools, skills or trigger-backed habits.
- **Process accretion:** piling up instructions, hooks or checks episode after episode without pruning, until context and workflow buckle under their own weight.
- **Process theater:** adding a ritual, template field or boilerplate rule that creates busywork without preventing recurrence.
- **Wrong lever:** writing prose instructions for what a small lint or script would catch reliably, or building brittle automation for a soft matter of judgment.
- **Shadow pipeline:** improvising a multi-step tooling, harness or skill overhaul inside retro instead of promoting it into the main pipeline.
- **Product creep:** quietly fixing the product bug or refactoring application code inside retro, tangling product and process diffs.
